# Calibration

Calibrating a model means choosing its parameters so that its prices reproduce the quoted ones. This page calibrates
Heston to four smiles with [`calibrate`](@ref), then computes the sensitivities of the calibrated parameters to the
quotes with [`calibrated_parameters`](@ref), by automatic differentiation through the calibration, without
recalibrating and without assembling a single Jacobian by hand. It follows the
[calibration page of FinancialFFT.jl](https://rcalxrc08.github.io/FinancialFFT.jl/calibration/), which writes every step
out; the test suite checks that the two give the same parameters and the same sensitivities.

## The problem

The market quotes prices ``m_1, \dots, m_n`` of European options with strikes ``K_i`` and maturities ``T_i``: here 13
strikes on each of four maturities, out of the money (puts below the forward, calls above it), whose prices hold no
intrinsic value. Their implied volatilities ``v_i`` invert the formula of Black and Scholes ``C_{bs}``:
``C_{bs}(S_0, K_i, r, T_i, v_i, d) = m_i``. [`MarketData`](@ref) holds them as a matrix, one row per maturity.

A model with parameters ``p`` gives the prices ``C_i(p)``, computed with the `pricer` of FinancialMonteCarlo by any
pricing method, one smile per maturity. Their implied volatilities ``\sigma_i(p)`` are the model volatilities, and the
calibration minimises the volatility errors

```math
e_i(p) = \sigma_i(p) - v_i, \qquad L(p, v) = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^n e_i(p)^2 .
```

Volatilities rather than prices put the errors of all the strikes on one scale: the price of a far out of the money
option is a small number, and an error on it of the size of the error at the money would be a large error in
volatility. [`vol_errors`](@ref) returns ``e(p)``, and `FinancialCalibrator.loss` returns ``L``.

Which quotes enter ``L`` is decided once, on the quotes: only out of the money options (an in the money option is
mostly intrinsic value, and its implied volatility is ill-conditioned), and only those whose vega at the quoted
volatility is at least `min_vega` times the spot (`1e-3` by default): a price far below the accuracy of the pricing
method gives an implied volatility that moves erratically with the parameters, and a loss that is not smooth.

The model is Heston's, with the variance ``V`` of the log-price mean-reverting at the speed ``\kappa`` to the level
``\theta``,

```math
\frac{dS_t}{S_t} = (r - d)\,dt + \sqrt{V_t}\,dW_t, \qquad dV_t = \kappa (\theta - V_t)\,dt + \sigma \sqrt{V_t}\,dZ_t,
\qquad d\langle W, Z\rangle_t = \rho\,dt, \qquad V_0 = \sigma_0^2,
```

`HestonProcess(σ, σ₀, λ, κ, ρ, θ, underlying)`, with no market price of volatility risk (``\lambda = 0``). The quotes are
generated by the model itself, with ``p = (0.5, 0.2, 1.5, -0.7, 0.04)``, and their prices are moved by up to 1%, which
no Heston model fits exactly.

```@example calibration
using FinancialCalibrator, FinancialMonteCarlo, FinancialFFT, FinancialToolbox, ForwardDiff, LinearAlgebra, Dates

S0, r, d = 100.0, 0.02, 0.01
zero_rate = ZeroRate(r)
underlying = Underlying(S0, d)
maturities = [0.25, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0]
strikes = collect(70.0:5.0:130.0)
forward(T) = S0 * exp((r - d) * T)

# the quotes: out of the money prices of the model, moved by up to 1%, and their implied volatilities
method = CosMethod(2^9)
heston_market = HestonProcess(0.5, 0.2, 0.0, 1.5, -0.7, 0.04, underlying)
function quoted_vols(T, row)
    calls = strikes .>= forward(T)
    prices = pricer(heston_market, zero_rate, method, FinancialMonteCarlo.EuropeanOptionSmile(T, strikes, calls))
    prices = prices .* (1 .+ 0.01 .* sin.((row - 1) * length(strikes) .+ (1:length(strikes))))
    return blsimpv.(S0, strikes, r, T, prices, d, calls)
end
vols = reduce(vcat, [permutedims(quoted_vols(T, i)) for (i, T) in enumerate(maturities)])
mkt = MarketData(maturities, [Date(2026) + Day(round(Int, 365 * T)) for T in maturities], fill(r, length(maturities)), strikes, vols)
nothing # hide
```

## The variables of the search

The parameters are constrained: ``\sigma``, ``\sigma_0``, ``\kappa`` and ``\theta`` are positive, and ``|\rho| < 1``;
and ``\lambda`` is not determined by the quotes at all, since only ``\kappa + \lambda`` enters the prices. A
[`Parametrization`](@ref) searches over unconstrained variables ``x``, with
``p = (e^{x_1}, e^{x_2}, 0, e^{x_3}, \tanh x_4, e^{x_5})``, so that every step gives a valid model, and ``\lambda`` stays
at zero:

```@example calibration
variables = Parametrization(x -> [exp(x[1]), exp(x[2]), zero(x[1]), exp(x[3]), tanh(x[4]), exp(x[5])],
                            p -> [log(p[1]), log(p[2]), log(p[4]), atanh(p[5]), log(p[6])])
start = HestonProcess(0.3, 0.25, 0.0, 1.0, -0.3, 0.06, underlying)
nothing # hide
```

The search starts from the parameters of `start`.

## Derivatives of the model volatilities

The model volatility ``\sigma_i(p)`` solves ``C_{bs}(S_0, K_i, r, T_i, \sigma_i, d) = C_i(p)``. By the implicit function
theorem its derivatives are those of the price, divided by the vega ``\nu_i = \partial_\sigma C_{bs}`` at
``\sigma_i``:

```math
\frac{\partial \sigma_i}{\partial p_j} = \frac{1}{\nu_i} \frac{\partial C_i}{\partial p_j},
```

and this is how the rules of `blsimpv` (FinancialToolbox) differentiate it, without going through the iterations of its
solver. The Jacobian ``J = \partial_x e`` of the errors is then one evaluation of the pricer in dual numbers, through
the characteristic exponent, the pricing method and the implied volatility.

## Levenberg–Marquardt

[`calibrate`](@ref) minimises ``L`` in two stages. An Optim optimizer (`NelderMead()` by default; any other, with its
options, as the fourth and fifth arguments) finds the basin; Levenberg–Marquardt then brings the solution to where the
gradient of ``L`` vanishes, which an optimizer stopped by its own tolerances does not reach, and which the
sensitivities below need. Gauss–Newton linearises the errors around the current point,
``e(x + \delta) \approx e(x) + J \delta``, and takes the least squares solution of ``J \delta = -e``. Levenberg and
Marquardt add a damping ``\mu > 0``:

```math
\left(J^\top J + \mu D\right) \delta = -J^\top e, \qquad D = \operatorname{diag}\left(J^\top J\right).
```

For ``\mu \to 0`` the step is the Gauss–Newton one; for a large ``\mu`` it is a short step along the gradient
``-J^\top e`` of the loss, scaled by ``D`` (Marquardt's choice, which makes the step independent of the units of the
parameters). ``\mu`` is divided after a step that decreases the loss, which is accepted, and multiplied after one that
does not, or whose parameters are invalid (the pricing throws), which is rejected.

```@example calibration
res = calibrate(start, method, mkt; parametrization = variables, min_vega = 0)
p = res.OptimalParam
(market = [0.5, 0.2, 0.0, 1.5, -0.7, 0.04], calibrated = p, rms_error = sqrt(2 * res.OptimalValue / length(vols)))
```

`min_vega = 0` keeps every quote, as the FinancialFFT page does. The root mean square error is about 6 basis points of
volatility, from prices moved by up to 1%. `res.ModelVolatility` holds the model volatilities at the quotes:

## Identifiability

How well the quotes determine the parameters shows in the singular values of ``J``: near the optimum the loss is
about ``\frac{1}{2} \|e + J \delta\|^2``, so a move ``\delta`` along a right singular vector of ``J`` changes the
volatilities by its singular value times ``\|\delta\|``. A small singular value is a combination of parameters that the
quotes hardly see. [`vol_errors`](@ref) is generic in the number type, so ``J`` is one ForwardDiff call:

```@example calibration
cache = FinancialCalibrator.loss_cache(start, method, mkt; min_vega = 0)
x = variables.from_params(p)
J = ForwardDiff.jacobian(y -> vol_errors(variables.to_params(y), start, method, mkt, cache), x)
F = svd(J)
(singular_values = F.S, weakest_direction = F.V[:, end])
```

The weakest direction, in the variables ``x = (\log \sigma, \log \sigma_0, \log \kappa, \operatorname{artanh} \rho,
\log \theta)``, is mostly ``\log \kappa``, with the volatility of the variance and the level ``\theta``
compensating it: the speed of mean reversion shows only in how the smile changes with the maturity, and four
maturities up to two years see it weakly.

## Sensitivity of the calibrated parameters to the quotes

How do the calibrated parameters ``x^*`` move when a quote moves? The implicit function theorem gives the derivatives
of the calibration without differentiating the optimizer. At the optimum the gradient of the loss vanishes,

```math
g(x^*, v) = \partial_x L(x^*, v) = J^\top e(x^*) = 0,
```

and where ``\partial_x g`` is invertible

```math
\frac{\partial x^*}{\partial v} = -\left(\partial_x g\right)^{-1} \partial_v g = H^{-1} J^\top,
\qquad H = \partial^2_x L = J^\top J + \sum_{i=1}^n e_i \, \partial^2_x e_i ,
```

since ``\partial_v g = -J^\top``. The FinancialFFT page computes ``H``, ``J`` and the product by hand. Here
[`calibrated_parameters`](@ref) is the calibration as a function of the quoted volatilities, with these derivatives
as its rules ([ImplicitDifferentiation.jl](https://github.com/JuliaDecisionFocusedLearning/ImplicitDifferentiation.jl)
builds them from the solver and the conditions ``g = 0``): a method for ForwardDiff's dual numbers and a ChainRules
reverse rule. The whole ``6 \times n`` Jacobian is one call:

```@example calibration
∂p_∂v = ForwardDiff.jacobian(w -> calibrated_parameters(start, method, mkt, w; parametrization = variables, min_vega = 0), vols)
size(∂p_∂v)
```

Its columns follow the entries of `vols` (column major: maturity first). The quoted volatilities depend on the prices
through the inverse of the vega, ``\partial v_i / \partial m_i = 1 / \nu_i``, so the sensitivities to the quoted
prices are

```math
\frac{\partial p^*}{\partial m} = \frac{\partial p^*}{\partial v} \operatorname{diag}\left(\frac{1}{\nu_1}, \dots, \frac{1}{\nu_n}\right).
```

A check on one quote, the put with strike 85 at one year, against moving its volatility by a basis point both ways and
recalibrating:

```@example calibration
i = CartesianIndex(3, 4)
bump(h) = (w = copy(vols); w[i] += h; w)
recalibrate(h) = calibrated_parameters(start, method, mkt, bump(h); parametrization = variables, min_vega = 0)
h = 1e-4
(implicit = ∂p_∂v[:, LinearIndices(vols)[i]], recalibrated = (recalibrate(h) - recalibrate(-h)) / 2h)
```

The implicit derivatives match the recalibration to the accuracy of the finite difference, and cost one solve and one
pass of dual numbers instead of two calibrations per quote. The parameters ``\lambda`` (row 3), which the
parametrization fixes, have no sensitivity.

!!! note "A smooth loss at the optimum"
    The theorem holds where ``g = 0`` and ``L`` is twice differentiable. [`calibrate`](@ref) reaches ``g = 0`` with the
    Levenberg–Marquardt polish (`polish = true`, the default), and the choice of the quotes keeps ``L`` smooth. The
    pricing method must give derivatives that are those of its prices: the `CosMethod` of FinancialFFT keeps its
    truncation range constant under differentiation, which is harmless where its series has converged (Black–Scholes,
    Heston here), but not for Variance Gamma or NIG at short maturities (FinancialFFT.jl#64): use `LewisMethod` or
    `CarrMadanLewisMethod` for them.
